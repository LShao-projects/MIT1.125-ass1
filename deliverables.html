Data and Methodology

Cambridge Street Safety Priorities — Data and methodology

Purpose. Help Cambridge transportation planners identify neighborhoods, intersections, and times for further safety review. The unit of analysis is one reported crash record, not a person or vehicle.

Source and coverage. The team supplied a CSV snapshot of the Cambridge Police Department Crash Log. It contains 15,508 records and 11 fields, from January 2, 2016 to September 20, 2026. Data were obtained from an existing public log; no new survey or personal information was collected for this site. Download date: September 21, 2026. The snapshot is static and 2026 is incomplete.

Preparation. Dates are parsed as Cambridge local calendar dates and times. Participant and injury counts must be nonnegative integers; missing or invalid numbers remain unknown. Street names are trimmed, capitalization and spacing standardized, periods removed, and common street-type abbreviations expanded. Intersection pairs are alphabetically ordered; both streets are required for the ranking. No fuzzy matching, geocoding, or imputation is used. Exact duplicate rows are retained because no unique crash identifier is available; this snapshot has none.

Measures and comparisons. Total crashes count selected records. Injury crashes have at least one recorded injured person. Walk / bike crashes involve at least one pedestrian or cyclist and are counted once even if both are present. Mode-specific series can overlap. Estimated hospitalizations sum the source field and are reported as people, not crashes or verified admissions. Injury share divides injury-crash records by all crash records in the selected group. Filters combine with AND and include both date endpoints; rankings use counts with alphabetic tie-breaking.

Geography. Estimated neighborhood names are joined to official Cambridge GIS boundaries. Of all records, 12,456 have mapped neighborhood names; 3,052 (19.7%) do not and remain in dashboard totals. The map shows neighborhood aggregates, not individual crash locations. Surrounding streets are context only, from OpenStreetMap contributors.

Quality and limitations. There are 3,441 records with all three participant counts equal to zero, and 771 where estimated hospitalizations exceed recorded injuries; these values are retained, and their definitions and reporting completeness warrant review. Missing locations and inconsistent naming limit rankings. Police logs may omit unreported crashes and near misses. Without traffic or travel-exposure denominators, counts are not individual risk rates. Partial years should be compared over equivalent periods. Observed associations do not establish causes or the effects of an intervention.

Reflection

This dataset supports a descriptive account of reported crashes: where records concentrate, when they occur, and how many involve pedestrians, cyclists, or recorded injuries. Comparing these patterns can help planners choose locations for field reviews and identify where additional traffic counts or site observations would be useful. The explorer makes those comparisons transparent by applying the same filters to its charts, metrics, and recommendations.

The data cannot establish which location is most dangerous per trip, because it contains no comparable driving, walking, or cycling exposure. Nor can it prove that a street design or time of day causes crashes, or that a proposed intervention will reduce them. Missing neighborhood assignments, inconsistent street names, estimated hospitalization counts, and possible underreporting constrain interpretation. The partial 2026 total also cannot be compared directly with complete years.

The appropriate outcome is therefore a shortlist for investigation, supported by clearly stated counts and assumptions. Before committing resources, planners should validate locations, examine reporting definitions, collect exposure information, and assess local conditions. These records support questions and priorities for review; they do not by themselves establish causal conclusions.
