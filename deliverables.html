Data

The CSV contains 15,508 reported crashes and 11 fields from the Cambridge Police Department Crash Log. Each row represents one reported crash. The records run from January 2, 2016 to September 20, 2026.

Downloaded on September 21, 2026. This is the original source file; cleaning happens in the browser. The website uses this fixed snapshot and does not update automatically through an API.

CSV fieldDefinition and unit

Number of CyclistsRecorded cyclists involved; people per crash.
Number of PedestriansRecorded pedestrians involved; people per crash.
Number of Injured IndividualsRecorded people injured; people per crash. A record with at least one injured person counts as one injury crash on the dashboard.
Hospitalizations (estimated)The source’s estimated number of people taken to hospital; people per crash. These are not verified hospital admissions, and the estimate comes from the source, not from a model built for this website.
Neighborhood (estimated)The source’s estimated Cambridge neighborhood; a category rather than a measurement. Blank entries appear as “Unknown / not recorded” in the explorer.

One crash may involve several people and more than one type of road user, so participant counts should not be added together to count crashes. A blank location means it was not recorded; a zero in a count field is a recorded value, not proof that reporting was complete.

The file has no records dated January 1, 2016; keep this in mind when reporting a full-year total for 2016. The 2026 records stop on September 20 and do not cover the full year.

Data Cleaning and Methodology

Cambridge Street Safety Priorities: Data Cleaning and Methodology

We use the Cambridge Police Department Crash Log to compare reported crashes across locations and times. The original CSV remains unchanged. The browser prepares an analysis copy each time the dataset loads, using the same rules for every record.

Data preparation

Street names are recorded as text, so the same road can appear under several spellings or abbreviations. Differences in entry habits may explain some variation, but the CSV does not tell us who entered each record or whether it was originally handwritten. Without standardization, crashes at one intersection could be split across several entries in the rankings.

  1. Read and check the file. The CSV reader preserves commas and line breaks inside quoted fields, trims surrounding whitespace, and checks the columns needed for analysis. We also check for invalid dates, missing numbers, incomplete locations, and identical rows. This snapshot has no invalid dates, unknown numeric values, or exact duplicate rows, so all 15,508 records are retained.
  2. Standardize intersection street names. We remove extra spaces and periods, standardize capitalization, and remove a leading house number when it appears in an intersection street field. Common abbreviations are expanded: “ST” and “ST.” become “Street,” “AVE” becomes “Avenue,” and “RD” becomes “Road.” These replacements skip the first word, so “ST MARY RD” becomes “St Mary Road,” not “Street Mary Road.” The rules also handle “STEET” as “Street” and “EXT” as “Extension.”
  3. Combine specified aliases and street pairs. An explicit alias list groups variants of Massachusetts Avenue, Monsignor O’Brien Highway, and St Mary Road. For example, “MASS AVE” and “Massachusetts Avenue” share one label. The two cleaned street names are then sorted alphabetically, so reversing their order does not create another intersection. Together, these steps reduce 3,613 distinct original ordered street pairs to 1,891 standardized intersection labels. These are name-based groups, not independently verified geographic points. Similar-looking names outside the rules remain separate; we do not use fuzzy matching.
  4. Prepare dates and counts. We read dates in the source format and preserve Cambridge local calendar dates and hours without converting them to the viewer’s time zone. Year, hour, and day of week are derived for the charts and filters. Weekdays are Monday through Friday; weekends are Saturday and Sunday, with no separate holiday category. Participant, injury, and hospitalization values must be nonnegative whole numbers. Missing or invalid numbers would remain unknown rather than being filled with zero.
  5. Keep incomplete records where they can still be used. The 9,555 records without both intersection street names are excluded from intersection rankings but remain eligible for the other statistics. We do not infer intersections from address fields. The 3,052 blank neighborhood values become “Unknown / not recorded.” We check identical rows but would retain them because the file has no unique crash identifier to establish that two matching rows describe the same event. No missing values are imputed.

Measures and comparisons

Total crashes count records. An injury crash has at least one recorded injured person. A walk / bike crash involves at least one pedestrian or cyclist and counts once even if both are present. The yearly motorist, cyclist, and pedestrian series can overlap because one crash may involve several types of road user. Estimated hospitalizations sum the source’s estimated people count; they do not count crashes or verified hospital admissions.

Injury share is the number of injury crashes divided by all selected crashes. Filters apply together and include both date endpoints. Intersection rankings use the selected count, with alphabetical ordering for ties. Hourly charts count crashes by the recorded hour of occurrence. Weekday and weekend charts show totals without adjusting for the five weekdays and two weekend days in a week. Findings state their analysis period separately from the interactive filters.

Geography

We match the source’s estimated neighborhood names to the names in official Cambridge GIS boundaries. This places 12,456 records in 13 named neighborhoods. The other 3,052 records (19.7%) remain in the overall totals but have no neighborhood on the map. We do not geocode individual addresses or plot individual crash locations. Streets from OpenStreetMap contributors provide visual context.

The map uses the selected dates, day type, road-user, and injury or hospitalization filters. When a neighborhood is selected, the other charts narrow to that neighborhood while the map keeps all neighborhoods visible for comparison.

Quality and limitations

We retain unusual source values rather than guessing corrections. In 3,441 records, all three participant counts are zero; in 771 records, estimated hospitalizations exceed recorded injuries. Only one record in each of 2016, 2017, and 2018 has a positive injury count. The completeness and consistency of those injury fields need checking before comparing injuries across years or treating zero as evidence that nobody was hurt.

Missing locations and unresolved name variations can affect rankings. The file has no January 1, 2016 records, and 2026 ends on September 20; comparisons should account for these date boundaries. Police records may omit unreported crashes and near misses. We have no traffic-volume or trip counts, so these results describe reported crash concentrations rather than the chance of a crash per trip. They can guide further investigation but cannot establish a cause or show whether a proposed intervention will work.

Reflection

Working with this dataset showed us how much location comparisons depend on consistent naming. Standardizing street abbreviations and aliases, and treating reversed street pairs as the same intersection, brought together records that would otherwise have been counted separately. This made the intersection rankings more useful, although the resulting groups still depend on the accuracy of the original location entries.

The data supports choosing places and times for further investigation. The intersection of Albany Street and Massachusetts Avenue stands out across several crash measures, while the 2–3 p.m. peak appears in both weekday and weekend records. These patterns can help planners decide where and when to observe traffic conditions. They do not explain why crashes happen. Without traffic volumes or trip counts, we cannot determine whether a location has more crashes because more people travel through it or because each trip carries greater risk. The decline in 2020 coincides with the pandemic, but this dataset alone cannot establish its cause.

Cleaning also left some questions unresolved. Missing neighborhood values limit geographic comparisons, and the unusually sparse injury counts in the early years need checking against the source reports. We would use these results to select sites for observation and collect traffic counts before recommending specific changes to street design. Evaluating whether those changes reduce crashes would require a separate study.
