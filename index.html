Data Collection

The team obtained an existing CSV extract of the Cambridge Police Department Crash Log. The public log is compiled from approved police crash reports; this project did not conduct a survey or collect new personal information.

The supplied snapshot covers January 2, 2016–September 20, 2026. Downloaded on September 21, 2026; this snapshot does not refresh automatically. One row represents one reported crash. The site displays aggregate counts, not names, contact details, medical records, or individual personal locations.

From Patterns to Questions

What the full 2016–2026 record tells us

Key findings

↗

Albany Street & Massachusetts Avenue

66 reported crashes, the largest count of any street pair in the record. Three of the four busiest corners sit on Massachusetts Avenue, which suggests a corridor pattern rather than four unrelated sites.

↗

People walking and cycling

2,114 crashes (13.6%) involved a pedestrian or cyclist, and 2,261 (14.6%) involved a recorded injury. Cyclists appear in 1,319 crashes and pedestrians in 817, against 11,731 involving a motorist.

↗

2pm–3pm carries the most records

1,177 crashes (7.6%) fall in this hour, ahead of the 5pm commute peak. The afternoon concentration holds across years, so it is unlikely to be an artifact of one period.

Recommendations

Practical next steps for further review.

Start the corridor review on Massachusetts Avenue

Albany, Garden and Vassar Street crossings together account for 161 reported crashes. Review them as one corridor rather than three sites, and check signal timing and crossing conditions along the stretch before choosing an intervention.

View supporting finding ↑

Collect walking and cycling volumes

Counts alone cannot separate a dangerous location from a busy one. Commission short pedestrian and bicycle counts at the top corridor sites so the 2,114 walk/bike crashes can be expressed as a rate rather than a total.

View supporting finding ↑

Observe in the field during the afternoon peak

Schedule observation between 2pm and 5pm, when reported crashes cluster. Compare conditions against a quieter hour at the same location before attributing the pattern to design rather than traffic volume.

View supporting finding ↑

Close the location-reporting gap

61.6% of crash records carry a street address instead of an intersection pair, so most of the record cannot inform site ranking. Ask CPD to capture both cross streets where applicable; this would widen the evidence base at no survey cost.

View supporting finding ↑

Missing Data, Bias, Uncertainty & Limitations

Missing Data

  • 3,052 records (19.7%) have no neighborhood assignment.
  • Incomplete street pairs are excluded from intersection rankings, but remain in other totals.
  • Traffic volumes and walking/cycling exposure are unavailable.

Bias

  • Unreported crashes and near misses are not captured.
  • Reporting may differ by location, road-user group, and crash severity.
  • Busy locations may have more crashes simply because more trips occur there.

Uncertainty

  • Neighborhood assignments and hospitalizations are estimates.
  • 771 records show more estimated hospitalizations than recorded injuries; source definitions need review.
  • Zero participant counts do not necessarily establish that nobody was involved.

Limitations

  • Counts cannot measure risk per trip without exposure data.
  • Associations cannot prove causes or intervention effects.
  • 2026 is incomplete; compare matching date ranges across years.
  • Street-name variations may split or misgroup locations.

What is in the data, what we did to it, and what stays hard

What the file contains

  • One row is one reported crash, not one person: 15,508 rows, 11 columns, Jan 2 2016 – Sep 20 2026.
  • Each row carries a timestamp, up to two intersecting street names, a street address, five counts (motorists, cyclists, pedestrians, injured people, estimated hospitalizations) and an estimated neighborhood.
  • Coverage is uneven by field: 5,953 rows (38.4%) name both cross streets, 12,456 (80.3%) name a neighborhood, 11,731 record a motorist.
  • The address columns are read but never displayed. A house number is close to a personal location, so it stays out of the site.

What preprocessing does

  • Parses the CSV in your browser. A row is dropped only if its date cannot be read — 0 of 15,508 were.
  • Reads timestamps as Cambridge wall-clock time, so a viewer’s own timezone cannot move a crash to another day.
  • Keeps blanks as unknown rather than 0, so missing data can never satisfy an injury, hospitalization or mode filter. This file has 0 blank counts.
  • Standardizes street names: trims them, drops periods and leading house numbers, and expands abbreviations after the first word so “St Mary Rd” keeps its saint instead of becoming “Street Mary Road”.
  • Merges a short list of synonyms checked against City records — “Mass Ave” and “Massachusetts Avenue” are one street, written 447 and 799 times — then sorts each pair so “A & B” and “B & A” are one corner. Together this turns 3,613 raw street pairs into 1,891 real intersections.
  • Counts exact duplicate rows but keeps them; with no unique crash ID, deleting one would be a guess. This file has 0.

What stays hard

  • The intersection ranking runs on 38.4% of records. The other 9,555 were logged at a street address instead, and stay in every other total.
  • 3,052 crashes (19.7%) have no neighborhood and cannot be placed on the map, though they remain in citywide counts.
  • 3,441 crashes (22.2%) record no road user at all, so they sit in the total but in none of the three mode series.
  • Street names are free text — Massachusetts Avenue alone appears under 16 spellings. We merged only variants we could verify and left near-matches apart: “Saint Mary’s Street” is not treated as St Mary Road, because we could not confirm they are the same place.
  • Even after merging, 1,097 of 1,891 corners hold a single crash, so the long tail is thin evidence.
  • Nothing here measures exposure. With no traffic, walking or cycling volumes, a count cannot become a rate.

How the numbers are combined

  • Filters combine with AND; dates include both endpoints. Rankings use counts with alphabetical tie-breaking, and ties are stated.
  • Partial years are marked, never annualized. The map shows neighborhood aggregates, not individual crash locations.
