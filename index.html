EVIDENCE FOR SAFER STREETS

Cambridge Street
Safety Priorities.

The problemCambridge's transportation planners need to decide which locations to review first for street safety. They need to know where crashes are repeatedly reported and when they happen, paying particular attention to injuries and crashes involving people walking or cycling.

How this site helpsUse the charts and map to compare reported crashes at intersections and across neighborhoods. Look at changes from year to year or patterns by hour, then choose places to visit and study further. The results can also help you decide where to count passing vehicles, pedestrians, and cyclists.

Intended UserCambridge Transportation Planners and Road Safety Teams.

Understand the limitations ↓

Data Collection

The Cambridge Police Department Crash Log is generated automatically from approved police crash reports, as described on the City's Vision Zero page. It records when and where a crash occurred, the road users involved, and reported injuries and estimated hospitalizations.

For this project, we used a CSV snapshot of the public log covering January 2, 2016–September 20, 2026. Each row represents one reported crash. We cleaned and grouped these records to build the charts and map; we did not collect new crash reports. The data were downloaded on September 21, 2026. The site reads this saved CSV and is not connected to the source API, so it does not update automatically.

The log includes crashes that entered the police reporting system.

From Patterns to Questions

Four findings from January 2, 2016 to September 20, 2026.

Key findings

↗

Over Time: a sharp fall in 2020, followed by a rebound

Motorist-involved crashes outnumber cyclist- and pedestrian-involved crashes in every year shown. They fell from 1,133 in 2019 to 602 in 2020, then rose to 862 in 2021, 1,026 in 2022, and 1,233 in 2023, above the 2019 count.

The smaller series also fell sharply in 2020: cyclist-involved crashes dropped from 126 to 57, and pedestrian-involved crashes from 91 to 35. Their lower counts make these changes look smaller on the shared axis. All three groups can overlap.

The fall and rebound fit the pandemic travel disruption described in Cambridge's traffic sensor analysis. Reduced travel is a plausible explanation, though this crash log alone cannot measure its effect. The partial 2026 total should not be read as another annual decline.

↗

Top intersections: Albany leads all three measures

Albany Street & Massachusetts Avenue has 66 reported crashes, including 24 with a recorded injury and 30 involving someone walking or cycling. It ranks first on all three measures among the named intersection pairs, making it a strong candidate for a detailed safety review.

Garden Street & Massachusetts Avenue ranks second by total crashes (49). Hampshire Street & Prospect Street ranks third overall (47).

↗

Time of day: weekdays and weekends share the same afternoon peaks

On both weekdays and weekends, the most reported crashes occur between 2pm and 3pm, followed by 5pm to 6pm. Weekdays account for 910 crashes in the first hour and 873 in the second; weekends account for 267 and 238, respectively.

Across all days in the snapshot, these hours rank first (1,177 crashes) and second (1,111), with 4pm to 5pm third (1,021). Overall, 76.6% of reported crashes occurred between 8am and 8pm.

The morning pattern differs: 8am to 9am accounts for 6.4% of weekday crashes and 2.1% of weekend crashes. The data shows when crashes occurred, but does not explain why the afternoon peaks appear in both groups.

↗

Neighborhoods: North and West Cambridge have the highest totals

Among records assigned to a neighborhood, North Cambridge has the most crashes (1,470), followed by West Cambridge (1,409), Mid-Cambridge (1,356), The Port (1,285), and East Cambridge (1,243). Area 2/MIT has 446.

The leading neighborhood depends on what is being counted. North Cambridge has the highest total crash count, while The Port has the most crashes involving walking or cycling (251). For a review focused on pedestrians and cyclists, The Port therefore deserves particular attention.

These comparisons cover only records with a neighborhood assignment. The 3,052 records without one remain in the overall totals but cannot be shown on the neighborhood map.

Recommendations

Steps Cambridge transportation planners and road safety teams can take to investigate crash patterns and plan changes to help prevent future crashes.

Check traffic volumes before judging safety progress

Compare annual crash counts with traffic volumes to see whether fewer crashes coincide with fewer trips. The 2020 drop and later rebound make this especially important. Use complete years or matching months when assessing progress, and track crashes involving injuries and people walking or cycling separately. Check the early injury records before using them to judge whether street changes have reduced harm.

Start safety reviews at Albany Street & Massachusetts Avenue

Start with Albany, which leads all three intersection measures, and include Hampshire Street & Prospect Street because it ranks second for crashes with recorded injuries. Review crash reports and observe crossings, turning movements, and signal operation to identify recurring conflicts. Compare nearby Massachusetts Avenue crossings, including Garden and Vassar, but check each site's crash patterns before choosing changes to signals, crossings, or street layout.

Observe the two afternoon peaks on weekdays and weekends

Schedule field observations from 2pm to 3pm and 5pm to 6pm on both weekdays and weekends. At each site, count passing vehicles, pedestrians, and cyclists and record conflicts such as turning vehicles crossing people's paths. Compare these observations with a quieter hour at the same location. Use what the team finds to choose and test measures that address the observed conflicts.

Measure travel at sites in the highest-count neighborhoods

Select sites for traffic counts in North and West Cambridge, which have the highest neighborhood crash totals. Include sites in The Port when focusing on walking and cycling, since it leads that measure. Match travel counts to the locations, time periods, and road-user groups in the crash records. Use these comparisons alongside crash histories and field observations to decide where safety improvements should come first.

Missing Data, Bias, Uncertainty & Limitations

Missing Data

  • 3,052 records (19.7%) have no neighborhood assignment.
  • Incomplete street pairs are excluded from intersection rankings, but remain in other totals.
  • Traffic volumes and walking/cycling exposure are unavailable.

Bias

  • Unreported crashes and near misses are not captured.
  • Reporting may differ by location, road-user group, and crash severity.
  • Busy locations may have more crashes simply because more trips occur there.

Uncertainty

  • Neighborhood assignments and hospitalizations are estimates.
  • 771 records show more estimated hospitalizations than recorded injuries; source definitions need review.
  • Zero participant counts do not necessarily establish that nobody was involved.

Limitations

  • Counts cannot measure risk per trip without exposure data.
  • Associations cannot prove causes or intervention effects.
  • 2026 is incomplete; compare matching date ranges across years.
  • Street-name variations may split or misgroup locations.
